How the router chooses a route

  1. Place the endpoints. Each click is snapped to a usable segment for the selected profile, within 100 m. Changing profiles snaps the original clicks again. The displayed offset is not a verified access path and is not included in distance or time.
  2. Check exclusions. Explicit barriers and applicable zero factor grades remove an edge for that profile. Other poor conditions increase its cost.
  3. Combine the evidence. For accessibility profiles, the weighted harmonic mean combines the applicable factor grades; a very poor factor cannot be hidden by several excellent ones. Grade caps are applied, and the result is rounded to 0–100.
  4. Minimize total cost. The router selects the lowest-cost connected path. Forward and backward grades can differ because ascending and descending slopes have different effects.

Combined grade = sum of factor weights ÷ sum of (weight ÷ factor grade)
Accessibility cost = segment length × grade multiplier + share of event penalty

Any zero factor makes the combined grade zero, subject to the confidence rule in the slope tables. Event penalties are equivalent metres of resistance, not extra physical distance. They are distributed across the segments of each source feature, rather than charged at every geometry vertex.

The map reports physical route distance. The accessibility-adjusted time uses accumulated cost divided by the profile speed. These minutes express comparative difficulty, not measured journey durations. The distance profile uses physical distance and its own speed.

For example, a 100 m wheelchair crossing with final grade 60 has cost 100 × 1.6 + 15 = 175 equivalent metres. At 3.5 km/h this gives 3 adjusted minutes. The route is still 100 m long.

Compare with distance-only draws an orange dashed shortest path between the same snapped endpoints, alongside the selected blue route. The reported difference compares physical lengths. Its time uses the baseline speed, so it is not the selected profile’s time on that alternative. The baseline does not certify accessibility.

Distance baseline

Shortest distance

Minimizes geometric walking distance without applying accessibility grades, barriers or event penalties.

The standard walking baseline chooses the shortest geometric path in the available pedestrian network. It does not apply accessibility grades, steps exclusions, access-tag barriers or crossing penalties. A short route is therefore not evidence that it is usable by a particular person.

Reference speed: 5 km/h.

Routing cost equals physical segment length. Time equals distance divided by this speed.

Provisional accessibility model

Wheelchair

Prioritizes continuous, smooth, sufficiently wide routes with manageable longitudinal and cross slopes.

Smoothness, width, longitudinal slope and crossing kerbs carry high weights. Steps, wheelchair=no, impassable surfaces and restricted pedestrian access are excluded. Width at or below 0.60 m and a raised crossing kerb also produce a zero factor grade. Unknown width, surface and slopes receive conservative provisional scores; they are not assumed ideal.

Reference speed: 3.5 km/h.

Provisional accessibility model

Blind or low vision

Prioritizes detectable transitions, tactile information, controlled crossings and predictable walking surfaces.

Tactile paving and crossing type carry the highest weights; kerb detectability also matters. A flush kerb alone is less detectable than a raised one. Explicitly absent tactile paving receives a very poor score, rather than excluding every such crossing. Steps remain usable with a grade cap and an event penalty. This profile does not model audible signals, guide-dog needs or every obstacle a cane user may encounter.

Reference speed: 4 km/h.

Prioritizes stable, smooth routes with gentle slopes, adequate width and safer road crossings.

Reachability mode

How to read the isochrones

An isochrone is an approximate area around network segments reachable outward from one origin within a cost budget. Every profile uses its own speed and the same directional edge costs and exclusions as point-to-point routing.

Budget in equivalent metres = cutoff minutes × profile speed in km/h × 1000 ÷ 60

Distance-only bands use geometric distance at 5 km/h. Accessibility bands use adjusted minutes: difficult surfaces and crossings consume more of the budget, and excluded edges cannot be traversed. Smaller polygons do not necessarily mean a person actually walks that much slower. An inbound journey can have different reachability.

Choose the bands

Starting time is the first cutoff. Increment is the number of minutes added between cutoffs. Number of increments counts additional bands after the first. The defaults 5, 5 and 2 produce 5-, 10- and 15-minute polygons. Zero increments produces one polygon. Use whole minutes: up to 12 bands, with the last cutoff no greater than 120 minutes.

Bands are cumulative and nested: a 10-minute polygon includes the 5-minute reach. Each colour matches its cutoff in the legend. Changing the profile or settings recalculates the current origin.

What the filled area does and does not show

A bounded network search finds reachable edges and partial edges, then rasterizes and buffers them on a shared local grid. The base grid is 20 m, coarsened for large results; the access buffer is 50 m. Separate components and holes can remain.

The fill is a cartographic approximation around reachable paths. It does not establish access to every parcel, building or spot inside the polygon; a buffer can overlap a wall, river or inaccessible property. It does not add off-network shortcuts to the routing search. At a small budget the polygon may end partway along an edge.

Download GeoJSON

The export contains one cumulative Polygon or MultiPolygon per cutoff, coordinates in longitude/latitude (OGC:CRS84), and metadata recording the snapped origin, outward direction, profile, speed, cutoff, ruleset version/hash, graph checksum, generation time and polygonization settings. Preserve this metadata when comparing results; graphs and provisional policy can change.

Why a route can look unexpected

  • Standard walking works, another profile fails. The baseline ignores accessibility exclusions. A profile can disconnect at steps, a restricted path, a very narrow section or a zero-grade crossing. The graph can also contain disconnected mapping islands.
  • The endpoints move when changing profiles. A formerly selected segment may be excluded. The tool uses the nearest usable segment within 100 m and shows the offset; it does not search for an arbitrary distant connected component.
  • The route is much longer. Lower resistance can outweigh extra distance. Use the distance-only comparison and inspect the factor and penalty tables.
  • Times are large or reachability is small. Missing evidence receives the listed unknown grades. Several moderately poor factors and crossing penalties can substantially increase resistance.
  • No route still appears after snapping succeeds. Locally usable endpoints may belong to different profile-connected components. The tool never silently substitutes a standard walking route as an accessible one.
  • A barrier shown in hazard analysis is still traversed. Hazard categories and routing costs are separate policies. Hazard severity is not a routing multiplier; read the hazard classification guide alongside these tables.

Data, uncertainty and provenance

Mapped OpenStreetMap tags supply surface, smoothness, width, access and crossing evidence. Crossing kerbs and tactile paving include nearby associated kerb information. Missing or unmodelled values receive the fallback grades shown above. Confidence records evidence completeness separately from accessibility grade; it is not a probability that a route is safe.

Longitudinal slope uses numeric mapped incline first, then a configured local DTM, a regional model, Copernicus GLO-30, GLO-90, or unknown slope. The global products are coarse digital surface models and describe terrain trends, not measured sidewalk slopes. A coarse estimate alone cannot confirm the zero-grade slope barrier: it keeps a severe low score instead. Higher-confidence or numeric mapped slopes can still exclude edges. Cross slope is based only on mapped incline:across, never inferred from terrain.

The network’s topology uses rounded existing geometry vertices; crossing lines do not automatically become connected at every geometric intersection. The demo does not model live works, temporary closures, traffic-signal waiting times or all personal requirements. Graph edges are available in both directions subject to profile grades; general OSM turn and one-way restrictions are not a complete navigation model here.
